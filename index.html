1Summary

AbstractSrinivas & Singh (2018) ranked 33 industrial wastewater sources near Unnao, in the Ganges basin, using the ratings of three experts and an interval-valued fuzzy AHP. We recovered the survey's raw laboratory data from the report the paper cites2 and asked whether the measurements alone reproduce the expert ranking. They come close. A transparent computed ranking with equal weights agrees with the experts at a mean within-area Spearman ρ of 0.75, roughly the same as a regression model trained on the expert scores (0.74); standard pollution indices reach 0.28–0.69. Agreement drops to 0.50 when only the parameters tested almost everywhere are used. The experts' own scores track the number of parameters tested at each source (mean ρ −0.56), so much of the expert ranking reflects how much was measured, not merely how much was polluted. In 2012 the sampled plants account for about half of the chromium carried by their drains.

1.1Main findings

  • The paper's raw data is public. The cited "UPPCB (2013)" report contains every lab table the experts used, and its CETP table matches the paper value for value.
  • Without any expert input, a ranking computed from the measurements comes close to the experts' ranking (ρ 0.75), roughly as close as a model trained on their scores (ρ 0.74).
  • Much of this agreement comes from how many parameters were tested at each source. The experts themselves scored sources tested more often as more polluting (ρ −0.41 to −0.80 in every area). The number of tests alone reproduces their ranking at ρ 0.56. On a common panel (BOD, pH, chromium), the computed ranking agrees at ρ 0.50.
  • In 2012 the sampled plants explain only about 53% (Loni) and 39% (City Jail) of the chromium in their drains, so part of the load comes from sources not in the data.
  • The groundwater criteria cannot be computed per source. No open source locates the individual plants, and well pollution shows no gradient with distance from the industrial areas.
  • Agreement with the experts is not proof of a better assessment. There is no measured "true impact" to test against.

2Paper method

2.1What the paper does

The study ranks 33 sources (tanneries, two common effluent treatment plants, drains, textile, meat, paper and other plants) in four industrial areas near Unnao. Three experts, from a pollution control board, an impact-assessment practice and a research institute, judged each source from the 2012 lab tables. The output is a score from 0 to 1 per source, relative within its area; a low score means more polluting (Table 10 of the paper).

2.2Steps

  1. Ratings. Each expert rates the importance of each criterion and the performance of each source on each criterion on a five-level scale (1, 3, 5, 7, 9).
  2. Pairwise matrices. Every pair of sources is compared. Equal ratings give 1; a gap of k levels gives 2k+1 (3, 5, 7, 9); the reverse direction gets the reciprocal. We confirmed this rule from the paper's Tables 6 and 7.
  3. Consistency. II = (λmax − n) / (n − 1), IR = II / RII; matrices with IR ≥ 0.1 go back to the experts.
  4. Interval fuzzy numbers. The three experts' values for a pair become [(p′, p); q; (r, r′)]: q = geometric mean, p′ = minimum, r′ = maximum, p and r = midpoints between them.
  5. Fuzzy weights. Row geometric mean of each fuzzy matrix, then normalisation by the sum of all rows.
  6. Defuzzification. W = (w₁′ + w₁ + 2w₂ + w₃ + w₃′) / 6 gives one local weight per source and criterion.
  7. Final score. Score = Σ criterion weight × local weight. Sources are ranked within each area.

2.3Reproduction

All 33 final scores of the paper's Table 10 follow from its printed weights to within 0.001; the weights add up to 1.08, not 1. The fuzzy weights of Table 8 follow from Table 7. Only the ratings of one criterion in one area (C3, Site-2) are printed; the other source ratings are not published, so the full fuzzy calculation cannot be repeated.

2.4What this work replaces

The expert rating of each source (the performance part of step 1). The paper's criteria and the within-area ranking are kept, so the results can be compared one to one.

3.2Coverage and gaps

  • Panels differ: sources were tested for 1 to 17 parameters. No parameter was tested at all 33; BOD at 32, pH at 29, chromium at 28, COD at 22. Drains were tested for a median of 17 parameters, meat plants for 1.5.
  • Only 18 of 33 sources were sampled before the monsoon, so C3 is missing for 15.
  • Flow is missing for 5 drains and 3 plants.
  • Upstream and downstream samples exist only along the Loni drain (3 sources).
  • Not-detected values carry no detection limit; they count as a pass.

3.3Corrections to the paper

  • The "TDS 145" of Sadaf Enterprises is TSS 145 in the report (Table 7.8).
  • The ACI Oils outlet BOD is 157, not 150 (Table 7.24).
  • Omega International's BOD of 353 is genuine (station SW-28), not a copy of the CETP value.
  • The paper omits the station register for its two Magarwara areas. For 11 of those 15 sources the assignment follows the report's station order and is not confirmed by values.

4Methods

4.1Criteria from measurements

Every method starts with one row per source. The paper's criteria are computed from the lab data (wqml.summarise).

4.2A. Learning the expert scores

A regression model learns the paper's scores from the criteria. The experts supply the training labels, so we need them only once. We divide each score by the mean score of its area. That mean comes from the training sources only, so a held-out source's own score never enters its target. We compared twelve scikit-learn regressors by leave-one-out cross-validation: linear, penalised linear, PLS, nearest neighbours, SVR, tree ensembles and Gaussian process. A nested test, with model choice inside each fold, estimates the procedure without selection bias.

4.3B. Computing the criteria

Within each area, we rank sources for every criterion. Worse sources rank lower. We stretch the measured ranks onto the full scale so their mean sits at the neutral middle. Missing values count as neutral, as does a criterion measured for fewer than two sources in an area. We scale ranks to sum to 1 per area, which gives the shape of the paper's local weights, then add the ranks with fixed weights: equal, CRITIC, or the paper's own. CRITIC weights come from the spread and correlation of the data. The paper's weights serve as a reference only. No expert rates a source. Each rank can be traced back to measurements.

4.4C. Adding river and groundwater

Method C follows B and adds the limit-weighted river load (C6, C7) and a groundwater model (C8, C9). The model predicts a well contamination index (fluoride and TDS against drinking-water limits) from the industrial load of the well's locality. We test it leave-one-locality-out, with nested model choice. A drain-to-river concentration ratio appears only as a labelled scenario: it is not a measured delivered fraction.

4.5Evaluation

The paper ranks sources only within areas. We therefore measure agreement as the Spearman ρ between a method's scores and the paper's scores in each of the four areas, then average the four values. We also record whether the most-polluting source matches; tied sources count as a set. We compute standard indices (Nemerow, HPI, excess × flow, TOPSIS) using the definitions in method_b_compute.ipynb.

5.2Choosing the model (method A)

Two-component partial least squares gave the lowest leave-one-out error: MAE 0.233 on the area-normalised score. With 33 sources, we expected linear models to beat the tree ensembles and the Gaussian process, and they did. The nested estimate is ρ 0.736 (MAE 0.225). PLS was selected in 29 of 33 folds. The model relies mostly on C4 and C10.

5.3Sensitivity checks (method B)

  • Weights: 2,000 random weightings give a median ρ of 0.69 (5–95%: 0.38–0.81). Equal weights (0.75) and the paper's weights (0.74) both sit near the top. The weights matter less than the measurements.
  • Single criteria: C4 alone gives ρ 0.71, C10 alone 0.66 and C1 alone 0.39.
  • Panel size: if we count failures and excess per tested parameter, ρ falls from 0.75 to 0.26.

5.4Panel size

Each source was tested for between 1 and 17 parameters. We ran two checks (method_b_compute.ipynb, section 5b):

Inside each area, sources with more parameters tested got worse scores (Spearman ρ −0.41, −0.80, −0.50 and −0.52 for Magarwara-I, Magarwara-II, Site-1 and Banthar, and Site-2). Drains and treatment plants had the full panel and were scored as the most polluting. We cannot separate whether they were also the most polluting from how much was measured.

5.5Mass balance of the drains

We summed plant loads (concentration × flow) for each receiving drain and compared them with the drain's own load (method_c_measured_criteria.ipynb, section 7). Chromium marks tannery effluent. BOD and COD also carry town sewage.

In 2012 the sampled plants account for about half of the chromium in the drains. The rest comes from sources outside our data (unsampled units, untreated discharge, dumping noted in the report). By 2016 the City Jail drain carried four times the 2012 flow and eight times the chromium. The 2016 survey therefore shows changed conditions, not a test of the 2012 loads. The river criteria (C6, C7) rank the sampled plants against each other, but they do not cover the whole load.

5.6Groundwater

In a leave-one-locality-out test, no model predicted contamination in wells better than the mean. C8 and C9 therefore stay neutral. We have coordinates for 27 wells. TDS, fluoride, Cr(VI) and conductivity do not fall with distance to the nearest industrial area (Spearman ρ between −0.18 and 0.51). The only significant value, for TDS, has the wrong sign.

6Open work

6.1What is still open, and why it matters

We order these by how much each item could change the conclusions.

6.2Panel size versus pollution · analysed

What
Different sources were tested for different numbers of parameters, from 1 to 17. Drains and treatment plants got the full panel; most plants got 1 to 4. Criteria that count failures (C3, C4) or add up excess (C10) grow with the panel.
Why it matters
A source can look worse just because more was measured. When the counts are taken per tested parameter, agreement falls from 0.75 to 0.26. The experts read the same tables, so their ranking may carry the same effect. Any ranking used for regulation must compare like with like.
Done
On a common panel (BOD, pH, chromium), agreement is 0.50. The experts' own scores follow the panel size in every area (ρ −0.41 to −0.80). The effect is real, and it sits in the expert ranking too (Results, Table R2).
Still needed
The same parameter panel at every outlet, in new sampling. Only then can we separate pollution from testing effort.

6.3Unconfirmed source assignments

What
The paper labels sources S1–S9 in each area but does not list the Magarwara stations. 11 of the 15 Magarwara sources were matched to the report's stations by order only.
Why it matters
One third of the labels rest on those matches. A wrong match pairs the measurements of one plant with the expert score of another, which lowers or raises agreement for the wrong reason.
Next step
Confirmation from the authors or UPPCB. Agreement in the Magarwara areas (0.83, 0.75) is similar to the other areas. That suggests, but does not prove, that the order is right.

6.4Value choices still open

What
The mapping of industry type to the effluent scale (C5), the toxicity factors in C10 and the pH range are placeholders.
Why it matters
These are judgments, not measurements, and they change the ranking. A method that claims "no expert input" must state them openly. Regulators or domain experts must agree them once.
Next step
Review by the pollution control board or a domain expert, then fix them in wqml.py.

6.5Groundwater per source

What
Two of the ten criteria (C8, C9) concern groundwater. No open source gives the locations of the individual plants. Well pollution shows no gradient with distance from the industrial areas.
Why it matters
Without them the ranking covers effluent and river impact only. Groundwater is the main exposure route for people near tanneries (chromium).
Next step
Plant and outlet coordinates, from field GPS or UPPCB consent records, and wells paired near and away from each source.

6.6Drain mass balance · analysed

What
The 2016 joint survey gives flow, chemistry and metals at the Loni and City Jail drains.
Why it matters
That makes a mass-balance check possible. Sum the plant loads computed for C6/C7, then ask whether they match what the drains carry to the Ganga.
Done
In 2012 the sampled plants explain 53% (Loni) and 39% (City Jail) of the drain chromium. The 2016 survey shows much larger City Jail loads, so conditions changed (Table R3).
Still needed
Flow and chemistry for the unsampled units that discharge to each drain.

6.7No independent test of "impact"

What
Every result measures agreement with three experts. There is no measured outcome (river change, health data) per source to test against.
Why it matters
A ranking that matches the experts can still be wrong in the same way they were. Only an independent outcome can show which ranking is right.
Next step
Repeated paired monitoring for at least a year: upstream and downstream of each outfall, near and control wells.
