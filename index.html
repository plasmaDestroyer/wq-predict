Replacing expert judgment with lab data: a re-analysis of the Unnao industrial wastewater ranking

Re-analysis of Srinivas & Singh (2018)1 · code and data: github.com/plasmaDestroyer/wq-predict

AbstractSrinivas & Singh (2018) ranked 33 industrial wastewater sources near Unnao, Ganges basin, with the ratings of three experts and an interval-valued fuzzy AHP. We recovered the survey's raw laboratory data from the report the paper cites2 and tested whether measurements alone reproduce the expert ranking. A transparent computed ranking with equal weights agrees with the experts at a mean within-area Spearman ρ of 0.75; a regression model trained on the expert scores reaches 0.74; standard pollution indices reach 0.28–0.69. The agreement falls to 0.50 when only the parameters tested almost everywhere are used, and the experts' own scores follow the number of parameters tested at each source (mean ρ −0.56): much of the expert ranking reflects how much was measured, not only how much was polluted. In 2012 the sampled plants account for about half of the chromium carried by their drains.

Main findings

  • The paper's raw data is public: the cited "UPPCB (2013)" report contains every lab table the experts used. Its CETP table matches the paper value for value.
  • Without any expert input, a ranking computed from the measurements comes close to the experts' ranking (ρ 0.75), as close as a model trained on their scores (ρ 0.74).
  • Much of this agreement comes from how many parameters were tested at each source. Sources tested more often were scored as more polluting by the experts themselves (ρ −0.41 to −0.80 in every area); the number of tests alone reproduces their ranking at ρ 0.56. On a common panel (BOD, pH, chromium) the computed ranking agrees at ρ 0.50.
  • The sampled plants explain only about 53% (Loni) and 39% (City Jail) of the chromium in their drains in 2012, so part of the load comes from sources not in the data.
  • The groundwater criteria cannot be computed per source: no open source locates the individual plants, and well pollution shows no gradient with distance from the industrial areas.
  • Agreement with the experts is not proof of a better assessment. There is no measured "true impact" to test against.

What the paper does

The study ranks 33 sources (tanneries, two common effluent treatment plants, drains, textile, meat, paper and other plants) in four industrial areas near Unnao. Three experts, from a pollution control board, an impact-assessment practice and a research institute, judged each source from the 2012 lab tables. The output is a score from 0 to 1 per source, relative within its area; a low score means more polluting (Table 10 of the paper).

Steps

  1. Ratings. Each expert rates the importance of each criterion and the performance of each source on each criterion on a five-level scale (1, 3, 5, 7, 9).
  2. Pairwise matrices. Every pair of sources is compared. Equal ratings give 1; a gap of k levels gives 2k+1 (3, 5, 7, 9); the reverse direction gets the reciprocal. We confirmed this rule from the paper's Tables 6 and 7.
  3. Consistency. II = (λmax − n) / (n − 1), IR = II / RII; matrices with IR ≥ 0.1 go back to the experts.
  4. Interval fuzzy numbers. The three experts' values for a pair become [(p′, p); q; (r, r′)]: q = geometric mean, p′ = minimum, r′ = maximum, p and r = midpoints between them.
  5. Fuzzy weights. Row geometric mean of each fuzzy matrix, then normalisation by the sum of all rows.
  6. Defuzzification. W = (w₁′ + w₁ + 2w₂ + w₃ + w₃′) / 6 gives one local weight per source and criterion.
  7. Final score. Score = Σ criterion weight × local weight. Sources are ranked within each area.

Reproduction

All 33 final scores of the paper's Table 10 follow from its printed weights to within 0.001; the weights add up to 1.08, not 1. The fuzzy weights of Table 8 follow from Table 7. Only the ratings of one criterion in one area (C3, Site-2) are printed; the other source ratings are not published, so the full fuzzy calculation cannot be repeated.

What this work replaces

The expert rating of each source (the performance part of step 1). The paper's criteria and the within-area ranking are kept, so the results can be compared one to one.

Coverage and gaps

  • Panels differ: sources were tested for 1 to 17 parameters. No parameter was tested at all 33; BOD at 32, pH at 29, chromium at 28, COD at 22. Drains were tested for a median of 17 parameters, meat plants for 1.5.
  • Only 18 of 33 sources were sampled before the monsoon, so C3 is missing for 15.
  • Flow is missing for 5 drains and 3 plants.
  • Upstream and downstream samples exist only along the Loni drain (3 sources).
  • Not-detected values carry no detection limit; they count as a pass.

Corrections to the paper

  • The "TDS 145" of Sadaf Enterprises is TSS 145 in the report (Table 7.8).
  • The ACI Oils outlet BOD is 157, not 150 (Table 7.24).
  • Omega International's BOD of 353 is genuine (station SW-28), not a copy of the CETP value.
  • The paper omits the station register for its two Magarwara areas. For 11 of those 15 sources the assignment follows the report's station order and is not confirmed by values.

A. Learning the expert scores

A regression model learns the paper's scores from the criteria, so the experts are needed once, for the training labels. The target is each score divided by the mean score of its area, computed from the training sources only, so a held-out source's own score never enters its target. Twelve scikit-learn regressors (linear, penalised linear, PLS, nearest neighbours, SVR, tree ensembles, Gaussian process) were compared by leave-one-out cross-validation. A nested test (model choice inside each fold) estimates the procedure without selection bias.

B. Computing the criteria

For each criterion, sources are ranked within their area (worse = lower). Measured ranks are stretched onto the full scale so that their mean is the neutral middle; missing values are neutral; a criterion measured for fewer than two sources in an area is neutral there. Ranks are scaled to sum to 1 per area, the shape of the paper's local weights, and added with fixed weights: equal, CRITIC (from the spread and correlation of the data), or the paper's (reference only). No expert rates a source, and each rank can be traced to measurements.

C. Adding river and groundwater

As B, plus the limit-weighted river load (C6, C7) and a groundwater model (C8, C9) that predicts a well contamination index (fluoride and TDS against drinking-water limits) from the industrial load of the well's locality, tested leave-one-locality-out with nested model choice. A drain-to-river concentration ratio is reported only as a labelled scenario: it is not a measured delivered fraction.

Evaluation

The paper ranks only within areas, so agreement is the Spearman ρ between a method's scores and the paper's within each of the four areas, averaged, plus whether the most-polluting source matches (tied sources count as a set). Standard indices (Nemerow, HPI, excess × flow, TOPSIS) are computed with the definitions in method_b_compute.ipynb.

Model choice (method A)

Partial least squares with two components had the lowest leave-one-out error (MAE 0.233 on the area-normalised score). Linear models outperformed tree ensembles and the Gaussian process, as expected with 33 sources. The nested estimate is ρ 0.736 (MAE 0.225); PLS was selected in 29 of 33 folds. The model leans mostly on C4 and C10.

Sensitivity (method B)

  • Weights: 2,000 random weightings give a median ρ of 0.69 (5–95%: 0.38–0.81). Equal weights (0.75) and the paper's weights (0.74) are near the top, so the weights matter less than the measurements.
  • Single criteria: C4 alone gives ρ 0.71, C10 alone 0.66, C1 alone 0.39.
  • Panel size: counting failures and excess per tested parameter lowers ρ from 0.75 to 0.26.

In 2012 the sampled plants account for about half of the drain chromium; the rest comes from sources not in the data (unsampled units, untreated discharge, dumping noted in the report). In 2016 the City Jail drain carried four times the 2012 flow and eight times the chromium, so the 2016 survey shows changed conditions rather than a test of the 2012 loads. The river criteria (C6, C7) therefore rank the sampled plants relative to each other but do not cover the whole load.

Groundwater

No model predicted well contamination better than the mean in a leave-one-locality-out test, so C8 and C9 stay neutral. With coordinates for 27 wells, TDS, fluoride, Cr(VI) and conductivity show no decrease with distance to the nearest industrial area (Spearman ρ between −0.18 and 0.51; the only significant value, for TDS, has the wrong sign).

What is left, and why it matters

Ordered by how much each item could change the conclusions.

1Panel size versus pollution · analysed

What
Sources were tested for different numbers of parameters (1 to 17). Drains and treatment plants got the full panel; most plants 1 to 4. Criteria that count failures (C3, C4) or add up excess (C10) grow with the panel.
Why it matters
A source can look worse simply because more was measured. When the counts are taken per tested parameter, agreement falls from 0.75 to 0.26. The experts read the same tables, so their ranking may carry the same effect. Any ranking used for regulation must compare like with like.
Done
On a common panel (BOD, pH, chromium) agreement is 0.50, and the experts' own scores follow the panel size in every area (ρ −0.41 to −0.80). The effect is real and sits in the expert ranking too (Results, Table R2).
Still needed
The same parameter panel at every outlet in new sampling; only then can pollution and testing effort be separated.

2Unconfirmed source assignments

What
The paper labels sources S1–S9 per area but does not list the Magarwara stations. 11 of the 15 Magarwara sources were matched to the report's stations by order only.
Why it matters
One third of the labels depend on them. A wrong match pairs measurements of one plant with the expert score of another, which lowers or raises agreement for the wrong reason.
Next step
Confirmation from the authors or UPPCB. Agreement in the Magarwara areas (0.83, 0.75) is similar to the other areas, which suggests, but does not prove, that the order is right.

3Value choices still open

What
The mapping of industry type to the effluent scale (C5), the toxicity factors in C10 and the pH range are placeholders.
Why it matters
They are judgments, not measurements, and they change the ranking. A method that claims "no expert input" must state them openly and have them agreed by regulators or domain experts once.
Next step
Review by the pollution control board or a domain expert; then fix them in wqml.py.

4Groundwater per source

What
Two of the ten criteria (C8, C9) concern groundwater. No open source gives the locations of the individual plants, and well pollution shows no gradient with distance from the industrial areas.
Why it matters
Without them the ranking covers effluent and river impact only. Groundwater is the main exposure route for people near tanneries (chromium).
Next step
Plant and outlet coordinates (field GPS or UPPCB consent records), and wells paired near and away from each source.

5Drain mass balance · analysed

What
The 2016 joint survey gives flow, chemistry and metals at the Loni and City Jail drains.
Why it matters
It allows a mass-balance check: does the sum of the plant loads computed for C6/C7 match what the drains carry to the Ganga?
Done
In 2012 the sampled plants explain 53% (Loni) and 39% (City Jail) of the drain chromium. The 2016 survey shows much larger City Jail loads, so conditions changed (Table R3).
Still needed
Flow and chemistry for the unsampled units discharging to each drain.

6No independent test of "impact"

What
All results measure agreement with three experts. There is no measured outcome (river change, health data) per source to test against.
Why it matters
A ranking that matches the experts can still be wrong in the same way they were. Only an independent outcome can show which ranking is right.
Next step
Repeated paired monitoring (upstream and downstream of each outfall, near and control wells) for at least a year.
